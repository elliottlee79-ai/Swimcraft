SWIM Confidence

The bar under your feed bar, what fills it, and what it is worth.

Last updated: August 2026

Every angler knows the feeling of a swim switching on — an hour of nothing, then small fish, then better fish, then the one you came for. Swimcraft models that directly. The bar marked Swim is how warmed-up the water in front of you is, and it is the single biggest thing standing between you and a big fish.

What the bar is telling you

It runs from cold to switched on, and it names where it is:

  • quietNothing happening yet. This is where every swim starts.
  • buildingBait is down and something is taking notice.
  • on the feedFish are competing. Bites come more freely and the stamp starts to lift.
  • confidentA properly primed swim. The better fish are now genuinely on the cards.
  • frenzyAs hot as it gets. This is where the biggest fish — and the rewards — live.
It belongs to the peg, not to you. Move to another swim and you start cold again, and the new swim gets its own mood for the day. An hour of patient baiting is an hour invested here — which is exactly the decision a real angler makes when they think about moving.

Feeding is the engine — not catching

This is the part most players get backwards. Catching fish helps, but bait in the water is what actually builds a swim, and it is the only thing that builds one when nothing is biting.

  • Your feed washes out over roughly two minutes. Left alone, it goes to nothing and the swim goes cold with it.
  • A half-full feed bar is not enough. The swim will still slide backwards. Keeping confidence climbing means keeping the feed bar high — topped up little and often, not one throw and a wait.
  • A completely dead swim bleeds twice as fast as one with bait still working.
  • And the trap: the hotter the swim gets, the faster your bait goes. More fish, more mouths. A frenzy you stop feeding is a frenzy that ends.

But do not pile it in

Five throws in quick succession and you have overdone it. The swim spooks, the feed goes backwards, and confidence takes a hard knock off the top. Little and often genuinely is the answer.

Landing a specimenSpends some of it back — deliberately (see below)
Losing a fish is the one to take seriously. A lost two-pounder costs you roughly what nine or ten small fish earned you. Fumbling the strike is about a fifth as bad. It is worth fishing a hooked fish patiently rather than bullying it — not because the fish is precious, but because losing it takes the swim down with it.

Why landing a monster knocks the bar down

That dip is intended, and it is not a punishment. A big capture settles a swim — the commotion, the fish gone from the spot. Landing a specimen spends up to half of what you had built, so monsters cannot be chained. You rebuild, and you earn the next one. (The bar shows the drop without the warning flash, because it is a good thing.)

What a hot swim actually buys

Two things, and the second is the important one:

  • A gentle tilt in stamp. As confidence builds, the fish you hook trend bigger within what the venue holds. It is a nudge, not a switch — small fish never stop showing up.
  • Whether a specimen will take at all. This is the big one. Confidence is the dominant term in whether the venue's proper fish even considers your bait. Conditions, gear and season lend a little on top, but only a little — good bait alone cannot make a hard fish easy. A cold-start whacker needs a primed swim or a red-letter day.

Cold water makes fishing harder overall, but it makes the big ones braver — winter is slow and its fish are large. And every session carries a mood you cannot see: some days it do, some days it don't. That is the game being honest about fishing, not the game being unfair.

XP boost tokens come off the top of the bar

Land a specimen fish of 4lb or more while the swim is at the very top of the bar and there is a chance it drops an XP boost token. The gate sits right where the caption reads frenzy — below that, a specimen never rolls at all. The chance is deliberately low even then, so a drop stays a surprise.

That gives the whole system a point: the swim you patiently built is the swim that pays you.

How to fish it, in practice

  1. Pick your peg and commit to it. Confidence does not travel. Hopping swims every ten minutes means starting from cold every time.
  2. Bait first, and keep baiting. Get the feed bar high before you worry about anything else, then top it up little and often — never five throws at once.
  3. Take the small fish. They are not a waste of the swim, they are the swim being built. Land everything.
  4. Play hooked fish properly. One lost fish undoes a long stretch of patient work.
  5. When it reaches the top, stay there. Keep feeding, fish your best bait and spot, and that is when the specimen — and the token — arrives.
  6. After a big one, expect the dip and start rebuilding straight away rather than moving.
The short version. One peg. Bait high and bait often. Don't dump it all in. Land the little ones, don't lose the big ones, and everything else follows.
